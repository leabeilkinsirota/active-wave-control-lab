Solution of wave acceleration and non-Hermitian jump in nonreciprocal lattices

Drop a localized wave packet into a lattice where waves hop more easily in one direction than the other, and it takes an unexpected journey: it accelerates, slows down, and finally settles into uniform motion, while its amplitude keeps growing. Along the way it can even jump abruptly to a new position, with no disorder to cause it. We developed a continuum theory that captures this whole journey analytically and matches simulations of the Hatano-Nelson chain, opening a route to controlling waves in nonreciprocal metamaterials.

Fast wave transport in two-dimensional 𝒫𝒯-symmetric lattices

How fast can a wave cross a two-dimensional lattice? Balanced gain and loss, together with controlled couplings between sites, let waves travel faster than in the ordinary (Hermitian) lattice, and faster in chosen directions. The catch is stability, since non-Hermitian systems easily blow up. We derived a simple condition on the control parameters that keeps the wave packet bounded, examined the topology of the resulting lattice, and proposed a topoelectrical circuit that realizes it: a step toward fast and stable wave transport in planar media.

Supersonic wave propagation in active non-Hermitian acoustic metamaterials

Can a sound pulse travel along a waveguide faster than the speed of sound, without reshaping the waveguide or placing anything in the way? We showed that it can. Active elements sealed flush in the waveguide wall sense the pressure and respond in real time, adding local gain and loss and coupling distant points of the channel. Balancing these effects, guided by lattice theory, keeps the system stable and parity-time symmetric. We measured the accelerated wave packet in an air-filled waveguide and mapped the trade-off between stability and speed gain.

Harnessing nonlinearity to tame wave dynamics in nonreciprocal active systems

In nonreciprocal active lattices all modes pile up at one end (the skin effect), so pulses tend to grow or fade and spread out on the way, even with dissipation present. We found that nonlinearity is the missing tuning knob: balanced against nonreciprocity, dispersion and dissipation, it lets pulses travel in one direction as undistorted solitons, for weak and strong nonreciprocity alike. We showed this analytically and in experiments on an active electrical transmission line, with applications in signal processing and energy transmission.

Controlled wave acceleration in 𝒫𝒯-symmetric and inhomogeneous non-Hermitian lattices

Non-Hermitian couplings can make waves in a lattice move faster than in its conventional (Hermitian) counterpart. In a 𝒫𝒯-symmetric lattice the speed-up is constant; making the lattice spatially inhomogeneous turns it into a genuine acceleration. A dedicated relation between the control parameters keeps the system stable, so the wave packet speeds up without growing in amplitude. We demonstrated the effect in a topoelectrical metamaterial, where operational amplifiers in feedback create the non-Hermiticity: fast signal transmission that can be tuned on demand.

Invisible tunneling through non-Hermitian barriers in nonreciprocal lattices

In a lattice whose couplings are stronger in one direction, waves pile up at one edge, an effect known as the non-Hermitian skin effect. Place a mirrored nonreciprocal segment between two ordinary lattice sections, and this pile-up acts as a barrier that waves should not cross. Surprisingly, under certain conditions a wave does reach the other side while the barrier stays dark, as if it tunneled through invisibly, regardless of the barrier length. We derived the effect for quantum and classical systems and observed it in an active topoelectrical metamaterial.

Physical Review B, 2025 – Read more

Gravitational lensing and tunneling of mechanical waves in synthetic curved spacetime

Near a black hole, gravity bends light toward it, an effect known as gravitational lensing. We recreated this phenomenon on a lab bench with a two-dimensional network of mechanical circuits, designed by analogy with Weyl semimetals whose nodal tilt varies in space. Some of the required couplings are unstable and nonreciprocal; active feedback provides them while keeping the network stable. A programmed funnel-like potential bent mechanical wave packets toward its center. Reprogrammed, the same network mimicked Hawking radiation, particles tunneling out through the event horizon, in close agreement with the predicted mass loss rate.

Emerging exceptional point with breakdown of the skin effect in non-Hermitian systems

Topological edge modes and the non-Hermitian skin effect both pin waves to boundaries. What happens when they meet? We coupled two non-Hermitian Su-Schrieffer-Heeger chains with both directional coupling and onsite gain and loss. Hybridizing their topological modes produces an exceptional point, a singularity carrying a half-integer topological charge. And while the bulk states stay pinned to the boundary by the skin effect, the competing non-Hermitian parameters can overcome it and fully delocalize the hybrid modes. Exact analytical solutions explain both effects.

Active control approach to temporal acoustic cloaking

Ordinary cloaks hide objects; a temporal cloak hides events, making something that happened during a time window undetectable. In optics this is done with nonlinear fibers or by modulating the medium itself in space and time. We realized a transformation-based temporal cloak for sound with linear active control: actuators driven by programmed controllers create the required change in the medium properties in real time, while the physical medium stays unchanged and the cloak can be reprogrammed on demand. Simulations of a one-dimensional water channel demonstrate the approach.

Real-time-controlled artificial quiet channel for acoustic cloaking under varying detection conditions

How do you hide a moving object from a detection beam that keeps changing its angle and frequency? We created an artificial quiet channel: a dead zone that travels with the object and lets it pass undetected. Simple monopole actuators in the middle of the domain generate nearly one-directional control beams that open and close the zone, based on real-time prediction of the field, while sending little disturbance back toward the detectors. The more control effort, the quieter the channel. Simulations in a two-dimensional acoustic waveguide show the cloak following changes in the detection beam.

Temporal negative refraction

When a wave enters a medium whose constitutive parameters are both negative, it refracts negatively: its phase and energy move in opposite directions. We asked what happens if the boundary lies in time rather than in space, by abruptly switching a medium's parameters from constant to dispersive. The answer differs sharply from the spatial case: the wave splits into reflected and refracted parts, in both positive and negative regimes at once. With the right dispersion, the temporal boundary becomes reflectionless while keeping both refractions, as we showed analytically and numerically for an electromagnetic medium.

Klein-Like Tunneling of Sound via Negative Index Metamaterials

In graphene, electrons hitting a high energy barrier head-on pass through it perfectly, a counterintuitive quantum effect called Klein tunneling. We showed that sound can do the same without copying graphene's spinor structure: the right constitutive parameters of the barrier medium give identical transmission, as simulations of a two-dimensional acoustic metamaterial confirm. With a suitable anisotropy, the tunneling becomes perfect for every angle of incidence. This omnidirectional Klein-like tunneling is promising for lossless, direction-independent transmission of classical waves.

Non-Newtonian Topological Mechanical Metamaterials Using Feedback Control

The quantum Haldane model needs couplings that break time-reversal symmetry, which in a mechanical lattice means forces that violate Newton's third law. Passive masses and springs cannot provide them, but feedback control can. We equipped each unit cell with an autonomous controller that applies programmed forces in real time. The resulting metamaterial reproduces the topological phase of the Haldane model, with chiral edge modes carrying waves in one direction along its edges, topologically protected.

Real-time steering of curved sound beams in a feedback-based topological acoustic metamaterial

Can sound be steered along a curved path in open two-dimensional space? Acoustic transducers embedded in a slab waveguide measure the pressure field and respond through pre-programmed controllers, creating an alternating impedance pattern that mimics the quantum valley Hall effect. By drawing reconfigurable trajectories of different shapes into this pattern, we guided topologically protected sound beams along them in real time, with the waves staying localized on the path.

Tunable and reconfigurable mechanical transmission-line metamaterials via direct active feedback control

Metamaterials usually owe their unusual properties to periodic structures built into them, which fixes those properties at fabrication. We turned a plain, uniform beam in axial vibration into a metamaterial by feedback control alone, with actuators bonded to its surface. At a single operating frequency, the controller sets the effective stiffness K and mass M: zero stiffness and negative mass to stop waves, double-negative values for backward waves, or double-zero values for perfect impedance matching, and switches between them in real time.

Active boundary and interior absorbers for one-dimensional wave propagation: Application to transmission-line metamaterials

Waves reflected from boundaries can spoil a metamaterial's performance. We designed two active absorbers for mechanical transmission-line metamaterials. A feedback absorber at the boundary removes reflections at any frequency. A feed-forward absorber inside the material stops waves at a chosen point with minimal back-scattering, a sink without any physical boundary, using two actuators that launch a nearly one-directional control wave. Both rely on an exact fractional-order model of the metamaterial that captures delays, dispersion and reflections, leading to fractional-order controllers.
